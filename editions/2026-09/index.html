New E-Bike Requirements May Cause Speed Bumps for Riders

🔗

This summer, the state of New Jersey began implementing new e-bike rules for all residents and visitors. All e-bike owners and renters should become aware of the new requirements.

Firstly, what is an e-bike? The law defines “e-bike” as both low-speed electric bicycles with a motor that only provides assistance while pedaling up to 20 miles per hour (mph) as well as motorized bicycles equipped with either a helper motor or an electric motor.

New Jersey requires that you must be at least 15 years old to operate an e-bike and have an e-bike license or a valid driver’s license. An individual may not apply for nor receive a motorized bicycle license if their driving privileges are suspended or revoked.

All e-bikes, including low-speed ones, must be registered with the New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission (NJMVC). Visit www.nj.gov/mvc/vehicletopics/ebike.htm to make a NJMVC appointment, complete e-bike registration materials and learn more about the e-bike license process. Motorized e-bikes—not low-speed ones—are required to have insurance coverage and owners should secure insurance prior to NJMVC registration.

If you are looking to rent an e-bike in New Jersey, you must be at least 16 years old. Renters are not required to be in possession of a driver’s license or an e-bike license. Out-of-state visitors who bring their own personal e-bikes into New Jersey must comply with the state’s rules. This means they must be at least 15 years old, carry a valid license from their home jurisdiction, register the bike with the NJMVC and get insurance for a motorized e-bike.

The state law requires all e-bike riders aged 17 and under to wear helmets. However, several New Jersey municipalities have instituted more stringent e-bike requirements, including additional helmet regulations. Riders are encouraged to review local municipal e-bike rules before visiting. Visit njbikeped.org for detailed guidelines.

Frenchtown Mourns the Loss of Rick Epstein, Historian, Author and Founder of The Sun

🔗

It is with the heaviest hearts that the editorial team of The Frenchtown Sun shares the loss of one of our founders, Rick Epstein.

The Frenchtown Sun was born out of a desire for more local news and Rick joined early conversations to develop our current newsletter iteration. He named The Sun and provided editorial content and suggestions up through our June issue, shortly before his passing.

The only one of us with editorial experience—and extensive experience at that—Rick was our guiding light. He would (very gently, as was Rick’s way) chide us for “spinach” content—pieces that lacked a human-interest element or intriguing hook. At every editorial discussion, we find ourselves asking, “What Would Rick Do?” His wisdom and guidance continue to provide support for The Sun.

On page 7, readers will find Rick’s obituary. While it details his amazing, adventurous life, it is hard to capture the spirit, humor and kindhearted-ness of a man who gave so much to Frenchtown—from his early years as a local reporter to his walking tours and history books. Rick’s last book is in support of the Frenchtown Elementary School Centennial, held later this month at FES, and covers the history of the school and its students.

As a community, we mourn and celebrate Rick who gave so much to this town. We encourage readers of The Sun to celebrate the life and legacy of Rick by being curious, thoughtful and kind.

Shade Structure Will Provide Respite from Sun at Kingwood Dog Park

🔗

The Kingwood Dog Park, an off-leash dog area located at 178 Horseshoe Bend Rd. in Horseshoe Bend Park, will have a shade structure erected for dogs and their human companions to enjoy a break from the sun in the coming months.

Meet Del Val BOE Rep Tom Loughlin

🔗

TOM LOUGHLIN has represented Frenchtown on the Delaware Valley Regional High School (Del Val) Board of Education (BOE) since January 2024. Located on Senator Stout Rd. in Frenchtown, Del Val serves Alexandria, Frenchtown, Holland, Kingwood and Milford. The nine-member, publicly elected BOE oversees the school’s budget, curriculum, policies and personnel decisions. Current challenges facing Del Val include an ever-challenging budget, artificial intelligence, cell phone use and declining enrollment. The BOE is focused on maintaining academic and holistic student excellence while keeping strict oversight of the budget.

Tom is the only candidate who filed a petition with the Hunterdon County Clerk for the single Frenchtown seat in the November 3 election for the three-year term that starts in January 2027. As the Frenchtown representative, Tom represents the community’s interest while fulfilling the duties associated with the BOE.

Tom has lived in Frenchtown since 2021 with his wife Brooke. Having retired from a career in non-profit management, his expertise in high-performance governance translates well to his leadership role within the BOE. Tom and Brooke moved from Summit, NJ, where he served on the Summit Education Foundation and raised two daughters.

These River Rats Go the Distance

🔗

If you spot a group of smiling runners along the river path, you are likely encountering the River Rats Running Club.

Formed 7 years ago, the club welcomes novice and experienced runners from the Delaware River region—from Lambertville/New Hope to Riegelsville and beyond. Founded by William Semon and run by a dedicated Board of Directors, the club also recently achieved official nonprofit status to promote and support safe and organized physical fitness in the region.

Members pay $35.00 in annual dues. Alongside regular meetups on Thursday evenings, Saturday and Sunday mornings, members participate in races together; recently more than 25 members raced together at the Quadzilla 15k Trail Race at Trexler Nature Preserve.

Frenchtown residents interested in joining the club can contact riverratsrc@gmail.com.

The River Rats are hosting the inaugural Mischief on the River 5k presented by Spanner Tree & Shrub Care, Inc. race on October 11 with proceeds benefiting River Rats Athletics. Registration for the race is open to all runners and can be found at www.riverratsrc.com/motr5k.

You Won’t Be Able to Buy These Plants

🔗

On January 20th, the New Jersey Invasive Species Management Act (NJISMA) was signed into law after more than 20 years of planning and discussion among scientists, environmental organizations, the NJ nursery industry, land managers and other stakeholders. This new act lists 30 invasive plant species (currently) that are now illegal to propagate, import/export, sell, or advertise—in other words, no one will be able to buy and plant these species in NJ anymore.

Invasive species degrade ecosystems by outcompeting native vegetation, changing soil chemistry, increasing erosion and devastating wildlife habitat—some invasive species, such as Japanese Barberry, actually make areas more likely to harbor large numbers of ticks, making them a significant health risk to humans. A 1996 federal executive order defined invasive species as “alien species whose introduction does or is likely to cause economic or environmental harm or harm to human health,” making this invasive species management act good news for New Jersey. In fact, the passage of NJISMA brings New Jersey up to speed with neigh-boring states (most of which have had lists of prohibited invasive species for many years) and creates the state’s first comprehensive framework to tackle harmful invasive species now, and in the future as the list of species evolves over time. Some of the species on the current banned list are well known problems in our area, such as Callary Pear, Tree of Heaven, Porcelain Berry, Running Bamboo, Japanese Hop, and Multiflora Rose. Other invasive species which have been commonly used in land-scapes in recent years are also on the list and may come as a surprise to some people—Norway Maple, Mimosa, English Ivy, European Privet, and Chinese Silver Grass, to name a few. These, too, can spread rampantly and cause ecosystem devastation.

See the full list of currently banned species below, almost all of which can already be found spreading in and around Frenchtown (species present locally have been noted with an asterisk):

  • Norway maple (Acer platanoides)*
  • Tree of Heaven (Ailanthus altissima)*
  • Mimosa Tree/Persian Silk Tree (Albizia julibrissin)*
  • Porcelain Berry (Ampelopsis glandulosa var. brevipedunculata)*
  • Japanese Angelica Tree (Aralia elata)
  • Japanese Barberry (Berberis thunbergii)*
  • Japanese Clematis (Clematis terniflora)*
  • Autumn Olive (Elaeagnus umbellata)*
  • Weeping Lovegrass (Eragrostis curvula)
  • Winged Burning Bush (Euonymus alatus)*
  • English Ivy (Hedera helix)*
  • Japanese Hop (Humulus japonicus)*
  • Sericea Lespedeza (Lespedeza cuneata)*
  • European Privet (Ligustrum vulgare)*
  • Amur Honeysuckle (Lonicera maackii)*
  • Morrow’s Honeysuckle (Lonicera morrowii)*
  • Purple Loosestrife (Lythrum salicaria)*
  • Japanese Crabapple (Malus toringo)
  • Chinese Silver Grass (Miscanthus sinensis)*
  • Eurasian Mater-Milfoil (Myriophyllum spicatum)
  • Oriental Photinia (Photinia villosa)
  • Running Bamboo (Phyllostachys)*
  • Callery or Bradford Pear (Pyrus calleryana)*
  • Common Buckthorn (Rhamnus cathartica)
  • Jetbead (Rhodotypos scandens)*
  • Multiflora Rose (Rosa multiflora)*
  • European Water Chestnut (Trapa natans)
  • Siebold’s Arrowwood (Viburnum sieboldii)
  • Japanese Wisteria (Wisteria floribunda)*
  • Chinese Wisteria (Wisteria sinensis)*

In Memoriam: Rick Epstein

🔗

Rick Epstein, 75, of Frenchtown died at home on July 20.

Born in Syracuse, NY, Rick was raised in Lawrence Township and graduated from Lawrence High School in 1969 and from Syracuse University in 1973 with a bachelor’s degree in journalism.

He held his dream job as editor of the Delaware Valley News in Frenchtown from 1977-1984. After becoming a parent, he worked as a managing editor at the Somerset Messenger-Gazette in Somerville, the Green Brook-North Plainfield Journal and the Highland Park Herald. He spent six years as a copy editor at the Central New Jersey Home News before coming to the Hunterdon County Democrat in 1995 where he was managing editor for many years. Rick contributed reporting and photography through 2015. Instead of entering retirement, Rick worked as a full-time library aide at Delaware Valley Regional High School from 2016-2025, where he also gave presentations to the journalism and history students, and photographed school activities for his write-ups on the school’s social media channels.

He was the author of two books on local history: Frenchtown: Fires, Floods, Fads & Felonies! A Newspaperman Tells the Story of a River Town (Palmetto 2023) and More Frenchtown: When You’ve Read Frenchtown and Want More! (Palmetto 2025) published with funding from the Fargo Family Foundation. He enjoyed sharing stories of local history on his Frenchtown walking tours. Rick was also the author of Rookie Dad; Adventures in Fatherhood (Hyperion 1992) and The Right Number of Kids (McKenna Publishing Group 2003). His funny-dad column appeared in more than 150 parenting publications in the U.S., Canada and Australia.

A memorial service will be held in Frenchtown later this autumn. To make a memorial donation in Rick’s honor, consider mailing a check to the “Delaware Valley Historical Society and Museum Inc.” at P.O. Box 173 Frenchtown, NJ 08825 (or, donating to Delaware Valley Historical Society via PayPal) or dropping off a check or donation of food to Frenchtown’s Open Door United Methodist Church Food Pantry at 16 3rd St., Frenchtown, NJ 08825.

History Spotlight: The Fargo Family Foundation

🔗

For over a century, the Fargo family name has been deeply rooted in Frenchtown. As chronicled in Rick Epstein’s local histories, Frenchtown and More Frenchtown, the family’s legacy in the borough began with patriarch Clarence B. Fargo. Professionally, Clarence operated a printing and publishing bus-iness in Frenchtown, running a mail-order flower-seed and bulb business out of a commercial building on Bridge Street, alongside his work documenting local history. Beyond his business ventures, Clarence was an active community member who engaged deeply with the local area, eventually authoring the foundational History of Frenchtown in 1933.

The family’s deep commitment to civic life extended proudly through the generations. Clarence and his wife raised three daughters—Edith, Marion, and Marjorie—who lived in the family’s Frenchtown home for over a century.

Throughout their lives, the family remained dedicated to local development and community well-being. The Fargo sisters were especially committed to supporting the local community, contributing generously to local churches, emergency service providers and animal shelters, with a particular focus on the welfare of women and children.

To honor their father and ensure their lifelong dedication to the region endured, the sisters established the Fargo Family Foundation. Today, the Foundation continues to enrich the Frenchtown community by awarding grants that improve the quality of life for both people and animals throughout the Delaware Valley. From Clarence’s early preservation of our town’s stories to the foundation’s ongoing philanthropic impact, the Fargos remain an indelible part of the fabric of Frenchtown.
