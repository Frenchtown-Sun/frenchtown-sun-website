Voting: What Residents Need to Know Ahead of November 3 Election Day

🔗

As residents prepare for the upcoming statewide General Election on Tuesday, November 3, they have several options on how to submit their vote: residents can cast their ballots by mail, take advantage of early in-person voting or head to the polls on Election Day. The Hunterdon County website provides a simple guide to voting at: co.hunterdon.nj.us/970/Election-Voter-Information.

KEY VOTING DATES & DEADLINES:

  • Voter registration deadline: new residents registering to vote or those needing to update their voter registration details must do so on or before Tuesday, October 13. Forms to register for voting in New Jersey can be found at the link above. It is Voting: What Residents Need to Know Ahead of November 3 Election Day important to note that these forms must be received by the October 13 deadline.
  • Vote-by-mail: The deadline to apply for a mail-in ballot is Tuesday, October 27. Instructions on how to request a vote-by-mail ballot can be found on the county Board of Elections website at: co.hunterdon.nj.us/2639/Requesting-A-Vote-By-Mail. Vote-by-mail ballots can be returned using the post office (no postage is necessary) and must be mailed at least 7 days before Election Day. Vote-by-mail ballots can also be dropped off at any of the 12 drop boxes located throughout the county or returned in person by 3 p.m. on November 2 to the Board of Elections office located at 112 Broad St., Flemington; a list of the dropbox locations can be found at: co.hunterdon.nj.us/1130/Polling-Places-Ballot-Box-Locations.
  • The period for early in-person voting is Saturday, October 24 – Sunday, November 1. Registered voters can cast a ballot at designated early voting centers across Hunterdon County. Locations for early voting can be found at the polling places and ballot box locations in the link listed above.
  • In person voting on Election Day: residents can vote at Borough Hall (29 Second St., Frenchtown) between the hours of 6 a.m. – 8 p.m.

WHAT’S ON THE BALLOT:

The list of candidates in the General Election can be found on the Hunterdon County website: co.hunterdon.nj.us/2949/2026-General-Ballot-Candidates.

Voter approval of the referendum allows the district to qualify for 34% state debt-service aid on eligible project costs, reducing the district’s share of the total project cost from the estimated and approved eligible cost of $926,000 to $611,160; this includes costs for equipment, installation, architectural/engineering fees and permits. The system is going to be paid for out of DVRHS’s reserve funds and will not raise taxes. All registered voters in Frenchtown can vote on the referendum. Spotlight on The Frenchtown Public Library

🔗

For more than a century, Frenchtownʼs public library has quietly served as one of the boroughʼs most beloved civic anchors. Like many municipal libraries in Hunterdon County, early efforts to establish a public reading room in Frenchtown relied on volunteers and donations from local civic groups and residents. Throughout the early 20th century, community members rallied to secure a dedicated shelf space for shared books, and organized book drives and small-scale fundraising events.

As the collection grew during the mid-20th century, the library moved through several locations in town, including dedicated storefronts. In 1938, the library secured a stable home on Second St. (initially operating out of 27 Second St. before establishing its permanent home next door at 29 Second St.).

The library is an affiliate, but not a branch, of the Hunterdon County Library system. In fact, the only two libraries in the county that are branches of the main library on Rt. 12 in Flemington are the North County Branch in Clinton and the South County Branch in East Amwell. The Frenchtown library operates independently from the county library with the borough supporting the salary of library staff as well as owning the building in which the library resides; a 2022 grant to the Borough supported repairs to the library interior. The Frenchtown library maintains its own collection while providing patrons access to county-wide resources, interlibrary loans and shared catalog systems through which residents can request books from the county library be delivered to the Frenchtown library.

As a member of the Main Library Alliance—a network encompassing dozens of public libraries across Hunterdon, Morris, Somerset, Union and Warren counties—the Frenchtown library offers local readers access to hundreds of thousands of physical books, audiobooks, digital downloads, e-books and research databases with a single library card.

To learn more about the library, visit the website which is hosted on the boroughʼs main website: https://frenchtownboro.com/library/. Library hours are:

  • Monday: 3:30 – 7 p.m.
  • Tuesday: 3:30 – 7 p.m.
  • Wednesday: 9:30 a.m. – 1 p.m.
  • Thursday: 9:30 a.m. – 1 p.m.
  • Closed on Friday, Saturday and Sunday

Tree Removal Regulations to Know

🔗

Our area has seen its share of storm damage, as it does every year, and fall is when many folks do tree maintenance to remove dead, dying or damaged tree branches and trees.

The public right-of-way for shade trees generally refers to the planting strip or strip of ground located adjacent to the street or between the street and the sidewalk. The Frenchtown Shade Tree Commission is responsible for planting, trimming, care, and removal of trees within these public right-of-way areas. Residents are expected to apply to the Shade Tree Commission—call the Borough Clerk for more information—before removing a street tree located in the public right-of-way, unless the tree is immediately hazardous.

For non-street trees, the Tree Removal and Replacement Ordinance that was adopted by Council in 2024 applies (24-919, now section 50-311 of the Borough Land Use Ordinance). In 2024, every municipality in the state was required to adopt a Tree Removal and Replacement Ordinance based on environmental frameworks established by the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection (NJDEP) to preserve mature trees, whenever possible, throughout the state of New Jersey and, when preservation is not possible, to replace them. The borough ordinance specifically applies to the removal of any large non-street tree—that is, any tree with a diameter at breast height (DBH) of six inches or more. In most cases, to remove a large tree, residents must fill out and submit a tree removal application to the borough clerk. The ordinance also spells out replacement requirements—these detail which species of tree can be planted as approved replacement trees, how large the replacement trees must be, as well as how many trees must be planted to replace each removed large tree (that number being based on the size of the removed tree). It is important to note, however, that hazardous trees (including dead or dying trees) and invasive tree species are exempt from this ordinance. For more details and information, the full ordinance can be found here: https://ecode360.com/47941029.

Frenchtown Elementary School BOE Sets 2026 Goals

🔗

At the August 25 Frenchtown Elementary School Board of Education meeting, board members Adam Blackburn, Michael Dermody, Rachel Howard, Julie Klein, Erinn Nakahara, Veda Partalo and Kate Nugent set their district and board goals for school year 2026-2027.

The district goals are:

  1. Provide a foundation for later success in education and life by ensuring facilities, resources, environments and opportunities for all students to unlock their highest academic and creative potential. By June 2027, at least 80% of students will demonstrate measurable growth in two or more academic indicators as compared to baseline assessments through the implementation of high quality instruction, targeted interventions and data-driven decision making.
  2. Increase student awareness of future educational, career and community opportunities by developing and implementing initiatives that broaden student perspectives, with a particular focus on broadening middle school students’ academic and cultural perspectives while intentionally developing character skills such as empathy, respect, responsibility and conscientiousness.
  3. By December 31, 2026, the board will develop a comprehensive climate survey of students, staff and community members to conduct by February 28, 2027.

The board’s goals are as follows:

  1. Build a strong and representative board by making opportunities for board service accessible, transparent and open to interested community members and develop a process for orientation and continuing education of board members.
  2. Create a feasible, community-informed plan for renovating the auditorium and determine appropriate funding options for moving forward.
  3. Increase FES participation in community events including town government meetings, townwide events and parades.
  4. By spring, 2027 review and revise district policy to establish clear, age-appropriate guidelines for student screen use across the district based on the informed participation of FES staff, board and community members, as well as a review of current evidence regarding developmental and educational impact of screens on young learners.

Enjoy Fall Color and Leave the Leaves!

🔗

Actias luna, or the luna moth

Local trees are turning glorious colors. In our area, peak fall color usually arrives in mid-to-late October—just in time for Halloween. Some of our most notable trees for fall color include maples (turning shades of red and yellow), sweetgums (yellow/red/purple), tulip poplars (bright yellow), birches (yellow), sycamores (dull yellow) and dogwoods (red/orange/yellow)—the list goes on and on. Some years, even northern red oaks (and other oaks) put on a show with wonderful shades of deep red—in 2025 they were particularly impressive.

But why do leaves turn color in fall? These colors have been in the leaves all season long; the green that we see in leaves is due to chlorophyll which is what plants use to capture the energy of the sun. The shorter days, less intense sunlight, and cooler temperatures of autumn cause the chlorophyll in deciduous tree leaves to break down, removing the green ‘mask’ and allowing the underlying colors to suddenly appear.

By Thanksgiving, almost all of the leaves will have fallen—but notably not the leaves of oaks, beech, ironwoods and a few other species that have what are called ‘marcescent leaves’. These trees hold on to their leaves throughout most of the winter, making them easy to pick out in the landscape. It is thought that marcescence evolved to protect winter twigs from being browsed by ancient herbivores that used to roam North America such as giant sloths (and it seems to help a bit with modern white-tailed deer as well).

But even as leaves fall to the ground, their ecological job is not done. In recent years, most gardeners have become acutely aware of the importance of ‘leaving the leaves’. Not only do fallen leaves break down over time returning nutrients to the soil, provide natural mulch which retains soil moisture and slows erosion, and supplies critical habitat and shelter for overwintering wildlife including butterflies, salamanders and other creatures. Some of our most glorious native moths, such as luna moths and giant silk moths, overwinter in cocoons that they have spun in dead leaves which then drop to the ground where they hide (perfectly camouflaged) and bide their time until spring when they will emerge and fly again.

—Randi Eckel

Helping Make Frenchtown Home: The Generations Behind Frenchtown’s Oldest Store

🔗

Standing prominently on Kingwood Avenue, Frenchtown Home and Hardware is the borough’s oldest continuously operating retail tradition. The store’s journey reflects the changing character of the borough across three centuries.

In 1884 George W. Eddy and his son Samuel purchased an existing hardware business housed in the three-story “Eddy Block” at 34–40 Bridge Street. In an era when Delaware River traffic, the canal and the Belvidere Delaware Railroad powered local commerce, Eddy’s store supplied residents with homestead tools, agricultural supplies and heavy building materials.

The Eddy family operated the business through four generations. In 1962, William “Bill” Eddy downsized and relocated the store to 47 Bridge Street. Bill ended the 96-year family dynasty in 1980 when he sold the business to Howard “Gene” Hale who is remembered by locals as a “true gem” who could help fix almost anything.

Renaming the store “Frenchtown Home & Hardware,” Hale moved the store in 1983 to 11 Kingwood Avenue—originally E.B. Apgar’s auto-repair garage. Hale adapted the inventory while preserving the tradition of old-fashioned personal service.

In 2012, Hale handed the reins to Mike Tyksinski. Modernized to serve today’s homeowners, tradespeople and DIYers, the store remains rooted in the community spirit established by the Eddys and preserved by Hale. In an era dominated by big-box stores and online retail, Frenchtown Home and Hardware remains a rare local treasure. Besides serving the Frenchtown community through the store’s retail operations, Tyksinski is active in the Frenchtown Professional and Business Association and supporting the local arts community.
