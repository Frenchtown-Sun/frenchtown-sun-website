Environmental Commission wants to control invasive vines with beetles

🔗

When you see leaves that look like this, you’re looking at trouble.

As the soil warms up this spring, seedlings of the invasive and terribly aggressive Mile-a-Minute vine (Persicaria perfoliata) will sprout. Also known as devil’s tail, giant climbing tearthumb, and Asiatic tearthumb, this vine grows extremely rapidly and can take over gardens and woodlands, overgrowing shrubs, trees and outdoor furniture and buildings.

Single plants can easily grow up to 25-feet-long or more in a single season. The almost perfectly triangular blue-green leaves are easy to identify, and the entire plant is armed with tiny, hooked barbs that will catch on clothing and skin.

How to control this plant? Pulling the seedlings when the plants are young is quite easy—although you might want to wear gloves to protect your skin from the barbs. A biological control agent, the host-specific Asian Weevil Rhinoncomimus latipes is available from the N.J. Department of Agriculture to combat this scourge. This beetle has been thoroughly researched and has been used to successfully manage Mile-a-Minute vine since 2004.

This beetle only feeds on Mile-a-Minute vine, weakening the plants and reducing their ability to set seed. Since this plant must grow from seed each year—decreasing the seed production is key to controlling it. The Environmental Commission is seeking funds to purchase some of these beetles with the intention of releasing them at the Frenchtown Preserve to help control Mile-a-Minute vine there. —Randi Eckel

Borough Council meets on the first Wednesday of each month at 7 p.m. Meetings are open to the public, and they are also live-streamed at: https://frenchtownboro.com/livestream. The agendas for upcoming council meetings and minutes from past meetings can be found on the borough website.

At its April 8 meeting, the council introduced a resolution to adopt the annual municipal budget for 2026. This resolution will be acted upon at the council’s May 6 meeting. Other resolutions that were approved by the council include:

  • Hiring the street-sweeping services of the City of Lambertville.
  • Authorizing the borough’s participation in the Hunterdon Area Energy Cooperative Energy Aggregation Program. Frenchtown residents will be automatically enrolled, but they can opt out by returning a response card that is being mailed to each home. The program uses bulk purchasing to provide lower rates to consumers. For specific deadlines, detailed rate comparisons, or to manage your enrollment, visit the official HAEC website at https://njaggregation.us.
  • Authorizing the borough’s Environmental Commission to apply for a grant from the Association of New Jersey Environmental Commissions. If approved, the grant would provide funds for our Environmental Commission to host a community event to educate residents about local stewardship and invasive species management as well as to use a particular kind of beetle on borough public lands and parks to manage an invasive vine called “mile-a-minute.”
  • Authorizing a pilot program for the Frenchtown Sun and to apply for a grant from the Fargo Family Foundation to support it.

The council approved ordinances that:

  • Authorize conditions for filming in the borough. The ordinance is consistent with the state’s Film Ready program, which is designed to help production companies connect with municipal officials;
  • Establish the salaries and wages for borough employees for 2026;
  • Approve the start of Creek Road improvements using a $410,000 grant from the state Department of Transportation.

Also at the April 8 council meeting, Parks Commissioner Bill O’Brien updated that the borough has received a grant from the county to purchase and install modular skatepark equipment in Borough Park in early summer.

Frenchtown is getting “age-friendlier”

🔗

In March 2025, Frenchtown received a $70,000 grant from the state Department of Human Services to build community partnerships, assess the needs of our older residents, and create an “age-friendly” action plan. That plan is expected to be on the agenda at the June 4 Borough Council meeting.

The age-friendly effort is led by AARP nationally to help communities become more livable for the elderly. Frenchtown is already considered age-friendly, scoring 61 on AARP’s national Livability Index. The nationwide average score was 50. But we could be even friendlier.
More than 45% of households in Frenchtown include at least one person age 60 or older; the median income of residents age 65+ is $67,596 as compared to Hunterdon’s average of $90,843 for the same age group.

During the grant period, Frenchtown created an Age-Friendly Committee that updated borough demographics, created an inventory of services available to older residents, conducted a survey that received 206 responses, held listening sessions with key stakeholders, and hosted two town halls with significant participation.
The community-needs assessment found that:

  • Older residents feel a sense of “not belonging” within the community and have limited access to information about news, civic happenings, and social opportunities, which seems to stem from the absence of a local news source.
  • The county offers plenty of programs and services for older residents, but there are fewer programs within the borough and limited awareness of them.
  • A “community connector” would increase the availability of services and programs in Frenchtown by serving as a “match maker” between local volunteers and older residents with specific needs such as home repairs, transportation, and food delivery.
    The borough government could consider the needs of older residents with regard to home renovations to support aging-in-place, promoting repair of sidewalks to support walkability throughout the borough, ensuring the safety and livability of the approximately 48% of Frenchtown’s households that are rentals, improving parking downtown especially near Borough Hall and the library and strengthening the library’s ability to serve as a local “anchor” for all, including older residents and children.
  • The borough is poised to use two grants to improve sidewalks and pedestrian access.
  • That many residents and local organizations are eager to support age-friendly efforts, including the Frenchtown School, local businesses, churches, and service nonprofits.

To continue the age-friendly momentum, the state is allowing the borough to use its grant to implement several strategies that can serve as the foundation for ongoing age-friendly efforts. These include:

  • Launching the monthly newsletter that you’re reading now.
  • A free Operation Blue Angel program designed to assist Frenchtown residents who are at risk of a medical or life-threatening emergency. The program allows first responders to enter the home during a medical emergency without delays or causing damage. To read more about Operation Blue Angel or to sign up, go to: https://frenchtownpolice.com/. Contact Chris DeWire with questions.
  • Earmarking funds for the borough to consider changes to current ordinances that would reduce the cost and burden of obtaining local approvals for medically necessary home renovations that would facilitate aging-in-place.
  • Establishing a Constant Contact borough news service. See here to learn more.
  • Setting up a municipal trust that would allow the borough to seek funds to support creating an age-friendly community-connector effort.

On June 4, the Borough Council will consider the Age-Friendly Action Plan, and may modify it and may pass a resolution adopting it. Updates on Frenchtown’s age-friendly initiative can be found on the borough’s website at: https://frenchtownboro.com/community-services/age-friendly-frenchtown/.

Planning Board reviewing master plan

🔗

The Frenchtown Planning Board has just begun a five-year review of the most recent master plan re-examination report (dated 2021).

What is a master plan? It is a blueprint for a municipality, outlining a vision for the future of the borough consisting of balanced economic, social, physical, environmental, and fiscal planning. While not a set of laws or regulations, a master plan provides guidance to the borough when making land use, zoning, and capital investment decisions.

The 2021 master plan re-examination report identified items to be addressed over the next 10 years, and the Planning Board will be discussing the borough’s progress on those items in the next few months at its monthly meetings at 7 p.m. the fourth Wednesday of every month, at Borough Hall (and streamed live).

Former ArtYard visiting artist to give Frenchtown’s “Jane Doe” a headstone

🔗

A careful observer, strolling through the Frenchtown Cemetery, might notice a small, metal, grave marker inscribed: JANE DOE. It has been there since 1996, but this summer it may be replaced with a headstone with her real name on it: Wendy Louise Baker.

On Oct. 26, 1991, the body of a teenage girl was found by hunters in Warren County just off Interstate 80 not far from the Delaware River. Due to decomposition, the authorities could not determine the cause of her death, but it was declared a homicide.

No corresponding missing-person report could be found, and although a drawing of her was widely circulated by the State Police, no one recognized her. Because of a large tattoo of tiger on her left calf, she became known as the Tiger Lady.

With no one claiming the body, Frenchtown’s undertaker Eddie Johnson arranged for her burial in the Frenchtown Cemetery in 1996.

In 2021 Andrew Sullivan, a visiting artist at ArtYard from Fair Oaks, Calif., learned about Jane Doe and visited her grave, “I would like to honor her by providing a gravestone to replace the current stake in the ground.”

Andrew explains, “I have tried my best to be a more loving and attentive person, to care for, not only the visible world around us, but the invisible one as well.”

Although Jane Doe had been unidentified for 30 years, the Warren County Prosecutor’s Office event-ually used DNA from a bone sample to figure out that the Tiger Lady was Wendy Louise Baker, age 16.

Wendy was raised by her stepmother until she ran away from Florida at age 15 before landing in Coatesville, Pa., where she had relatives. When she disappeared, they assumed she had run away again and was living her life elsewhere. Her cousin Cindy Baker said of Wendy, “I don’t want to say [she was] a lost soul, but she seemed like she was kind of bounced here, bounced there, you know.”

Wendy’s granite tombstone will cost about $750. Andrew said, “I am going to transport it across-country in my own car sometime in the latter half of June. It will be a pilgrimage!” —Rick Epstein

Borough considering parking meters

🔗

Turnover of parking spaces in downtown Frenchtown continues to be a significant challenge, particularly as warmer weather brings an increase in visitors. On weekends, many people park in municipal lots to access the D&R Canal Path, often leaving their vehicles for the entire day without visiting local businesses. This limits availability for customers who are looking to shop, dine, and support the downtown.

To address this issue, the borough is actively discussing the potential implementation of parking meters—an approach successfully used in many comparable river towns—to encourage greater turnover of parking spaces.

In addition to improving access to downtown businesses, metered parking would:

  • Help ensure adequate funding for police coverage and EMS services during peak tourism months
  • Create a dedicated revenue stream for downtown beautification and ongoing maintenance
  • Shift a portion of the financial burden from residential taxpayers to visitors who utilize public parking

This is part of a broader effort to balance accessibility, economic vitality, and quality of life, particularly during the busy summer season.

Zöe Tober wins school centennial essay contest

🔗

The winners of the Frenchtown School centennial essay contest are Zöe Tober, first prize; Joshua Sanchez-Quackenbush, second prize; and Rosalie Strasser, third prize. Zöe and Rosie are eighth graders and Josh is a seventh grader.

They were among nine students who wrote on the theme “The Next 100 Years,” under the helpful supervision of Frenchtown School’s Language Arts duo—teacher Beth McCann and aide Ken Nohe. Donated by an anonymous benefactor, $100 will go to Zöe, $50 to Josh, and $25 to Rosie.

The contestants made predictions and recommendations for the future of Frenchtown and of planet Earth. Personal ambitions were also shared. Some efforts were serious and others were touched with whimsy.

The students’ names were redacted so the essays could be judged “blind.” The judges were Morgan Taylor, owner of Country Chic and president of the Frenchtown Business & Professional Association; Scott Sheldon, co-owner of the Frenchtown Bookshop; and Rick Epstein, local history enthusiast.

“The quality of the essays was heartening,” said Rick, “and we’re going to include some of them in a book that will be a souvenir of the Frenchtown School’s centennial.”

Zöe wrote, “Frenchtown is a beautiful place to live, a town so full of love and joy. Why isn’t the rest of the world? Why do people hate? Why do we fight? I don’t know, but I do know that out of all the hatred in the world, it is usually caused by sadness. And once you start using this angry façade when you’re hurting, it can be hard to stop. That’s because it feels easier than talking through the feelings you’re experiencing.”

Her hope is that “one day the love in Frenchtown will spread to other communities, and keep growing from there until we have real world peace. As we move into high school, I challenge everyone here to be brave enough to choose talking over fighting.”

She urges, “Next time you see someone being mean, don’t just do it back. You need to help them correct their own behavior or it will never change. And I can’t stress this enough, but hate is rooted in sadness. You need to be an ally to the ones who are hurting, not an enemy. Peace starts now, it starts with you.”

Frenchtown then & now

🔗

Race Street is in better shape nowadays, and current parental “best practices” preclude parking babies on it. Note the National Hotel in the background on the right. The PRINTING sign marks the site of William Sipes’ shop and office of his Frenchtown Star newspaper, and indicates the photo was taken no earlier than 1879 and sometime before 1885, when Sipes’ operation had moved to the other side of Race Street. The buildings on the left were among the five demolished in 1954 and replaced by the yellow-brick building that wraps around the inside corner of Bridge and Race. The redeveloper was Edward Flecker, who did the plumbing for Hunterdon Medical Center and Del Val High School. The old photo is artificially colorized and maybe doesn’t do justice to the carpet the baby is sitting on. —Rick Epstein
