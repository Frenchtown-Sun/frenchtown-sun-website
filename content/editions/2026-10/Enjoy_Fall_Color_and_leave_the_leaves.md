Local trees are turning glorious colors. In our 
area, peak fall color usually arrives in mid-to-late 
October—just in time for Halloween. Some of our 
most notable trees for fall color include maples 
(turning shades of red and yellow), sweetgums 
(yellow/red/purple), tulip poplars (bright yellow), 
birches (yellow), sycamores (dull yellow) and 
dogwoods (red/orange/yellow)—the list goes on and 
on. Some years, even northern red oaks (and other 
oaks) put on a show with wonderful shades of deep 
red—in 2025 they were particularly impressive. 

But why do leaves turn color in fall? These colors 
have been in the leaves all season long; the green 
that we see in leaves is due to chlorophyll which is 
what plants use to capture the energy of the sun. 
The shorter days, less intense sunlight, and cooler 
temperatures of autumn cause the chlorophyll in 
deciduous tree leaves to break down, removing the 
green ‘mask’ and allowing the underlying colors to 
suddenly appear.

By Thanksgiving, almost all of the leaves will 
have fallen—but notably not the leaves of oaks, 
beech, ironwoods and a few other species that have 
what are called ‘marcescent leaves’. These trees 
hold on to their leaves throughout most of the 
winter, making them easy to pick out in the landscape. It is thought that marcescence evolved to 
protect winter twigs from being browsed by ancient 
herbivores that used to roam North America such as 
giant sloths (and it seems to help a bit with modern 
white-tailed deer as well).

But even as leaves fall to the ground, their 
ecological job is not done. In recent years, most
gardeners have become acutely aware of the importance of ‘leaving the leaves’. Not only do fallen leaves 
break down over time returning nutrients to the soil, 
provide natural mulch which retains soil moisture 
and slows erosion, and supplies critical habitat and 
shelter for overwintering wildlife including 
butterflies, salamanders and other creatures. Some 
of our most glorious native moths, such as luna 
moths and giant silk moths, overwinter in cocoons 
that they have spun in dead leaves which then drop 
to the ground where they hide (perfectly 
camouflaged) and bide their time until spring when 
they will emerge and fly again. 

_—Randi Eckel_
