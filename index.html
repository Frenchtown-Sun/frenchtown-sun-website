Borough Council to Vote on Prohibiting Data Centers in Frenchtown

🔗

On Wednesday, October 7, Borough Council voted in favor of introducing Ordinance #963, which would amend borough codes to prohibit data centers in all zones of the borough. The Council’s action followed recommendations provided by the Planning Board that were prepared at the request of Mayor Brad Myhre. In a September 2, 2026, press release, Executive Director of the Association of New Jersey Environmental Commissions was quoted as saying, “To date, at least 109 New Jersey municipalities and an entire county have taken action to permanently or temporarily ban data centers.” The Council’s action was prompted by a combination of factors: concerns expressed by borough residents regarding environmental impacts and electricity costs, actions already taken by neighboring towns (Alexandria, Kingwood and Milford) to ban or restrict data centers, and an ongoing national conversation about data centers.

Under the New Jersey Municipal Land Use Law, municipalities are authorized to identify permitted uses within various zones—such as residences, retail establishments, gas stations and schools. Although data centers are not currently listed as a permitted use under borough rules, rendering them disallowed by default, Borough Professional Planner Darlene Green noted that developers in other communities have circumvented local zoning by housing data centers inside warehouses. Borough Attorney Albert Cruz explained at a Council meeting that explicitly defining and prohibiting data centers provides a clear legal safeguard against such workarounds. Furthermore, the Planning Board carefully crafted the ordinance to ensure that it would not be deemed “arbitrary and capricious” in the event of a legal challenge. A key priority for both the Planning Board in its recommendations and the Borough Council in its October 7 action was addressing a pre-existing facility owned by Brightspeed. Because the facility provides phone and internet service, the ordinance ensures the facility would not be classified as a data center.

After the introduction of the ordinance at the October Borough Council meeting, the draft ordinance will return to the Planning Board for a “consistency review” to ensure alignment with the town’s Master Plan. If the Planning Board finds that the ordinance is “not inconsistent” with the master plan, the ordinance will be returned to Borough Council for a public hearing at the Council’s November meeting after which Council will vote to either adopt or reject the ordinance. If adopted, the ordinance will go into effect and become part of Frenchtown’s municipal code.

The draft ordinance is available on the Borough website at: frenchtownboro.com/local-government/legal-notices/.

Spotlight on the Frenchtown Cemetery Association and the Cemetery’s History

🔗

With its main entrance on Trenton Ave., the beautiful Frenchtown Cemetery is an integral part of local history and culture. It operates as a Mutual Cemetery Company under IRS regulations and is governed by the current Trustees of the Frenchtown Cemetery Association: Timothy Downing Brown, Charles Eichlin, Leslie Stintsman, Gordon Dragt, Susie Swider and Jerry Case. Case, who served as Superintendent for over 45 years, now holds the title of Superintendent Emeritus. The board also honors the memory of Dennis Carr, a devoted trustee of 24 years who passed away in February 2026; his wife, Patricia, carries on the long-standing Carr family tradition of placing seasonal and holiday flowers on gravesites.

The cemetery does not have a religious affiliation, and it accommodates a wide range of cultural, religious and traditional needs, offering traditional burial and cremation interments. As a small non-profit that generates revenue primarily through plot sales, interment services, and modest investment yields on its funds, all proceeds are reinvested into maintaining the property, with no private stock or dividend distributions. Consistent with state law, the cemetery maintains a permanent trust funded by deposits from a fixed percentage of every grave sale. The principal deposited into the trust can never be spent; instead, it is invested to generate income that, along with donations, is used to maintain cemetery grounds. The internal roads are owned by the Cemetery Association; and the borough has no responsibility for the upkeep or maintenance of cemetery property and roads. As a registered non-profit mutual cemetery association, the cemetery is exempt from local property taxes; an outhouse next to the toolshed and a handpump connected to a well provides sewer and water services.

Given its long history in Frenchtown and its important contributions to the borough, the French-town Cemetery welcomes visitors to view its grounds and explore its history while respecting the grave-sites of those who are buried there. Any individual interested in purchasing a plot or making a tax-deductible donation to the Cemetery can contact: Timothy Downing Brown at 908-752-0539; or Charles Eichlin at 908-996-2308; or email frenchtowncemetery@gmail.com.

Frenchtown Cemetery History

Frenchtown Cemetery originated in 1810 as a private family graveyard for the Mallet-Prevost family. The first burial recorded on the grounds was that of Jeanne Patry Mallet-Prevost, wife of Paul Henri Mallet-Prevost. For nearly five decades, the site served as the family’s private resting place until 1857, when the Prevost family established the Frenchtown Cemetery Association and opened the cemetery to the public. Following its public establishment, the first recorded public burial was Isaac F. Tomson (1835-1857), whose headstone remains standing a few hundred feet past the entrance gate along the left side of the main drive. Shortly thereafter, Jacob Rounsavell (1817-1857) became the second public internee.

As Frenchtown grew, the cemetery expanded to accommodate the community. By 1880, the grounds contained more than 500 graves prompting the installation of a distinctive stone fence across the front perimeter. In 1897, local builder John L. Slack constructed the grounds’ only frame structure—a wooden tool house—for $261. In 1901, the main avenue through the grounds was macadamized to improve access for funeral processions and visitors.

Among the most prominent monuments are those in memory of Private Garret Roberson, who died at the Battle of Gaines Mills in 1862; Robert Rittenhouse, who was “lost at sea” in 1935 when the USS Havana ran aground on a reef in the Bahamas; and of Private William Finney Jr. and other victims of a spinal meningitis outbreak at Jefferson Barracks in Missouri in 1907. Today, the cemetery spans approximately 14.5 acres and serves as the final resting place for nearly 5,800 individuals.

—Excerpt courtesy of Frenchtown and More Frenchtown by Rick Epstein.

Nurturing Curiosity and Community: Inside Secret Garden Montessori

🔗

Frenchtown has the benefit of two vibrant pre-school programs on-site within the borough. One program is offered by the Frenchtown Elementary School: the Little Paws Preschool which offers a full-day program starting at age 3 that is fully integrated into the elementary school curriculum. The second is the private Secret Garden Montessori, located at the Frenchtown Barn Theater at 1110 Harrison St., which prides itself on offering a child-centered approach to learning.

Secret Garden’s story began in 2006 when a dedicated group of Delaware River Valley families partnered with Montessori educator Barbette Robillard to form a small, home-based learning cooperative. As local interest and enrollment grew, the program formalized its nonprofit structure around 2009–2010 and moved into its current home in the Barn Theater building owned by the DeSapio family. Today, the school has the capacity to serve up to 45 children—including four students who reside in Frenchtown Borough.

At the heart of Secret Garden is the educational philosophy of Dr. Maria Montessori (1870–1952), an Italian physician and educator who pioneered a method based on close observation of child development. Unlike traditional school environments, a Montessori classroom emphasizes an active role for students, who work at their own pace in mixed-age settings. The school serves children in two distinct programs: a Toddler program for ages 18 months to 3 years, and a Primary program for ages 3 to 6. A typical day features a long, uninterrupted work cycle. During this time, children select developmentally appropriate activities, working independently or in small groups with guidance from staff who hold credentials from the Princeton Center for Teacher Education. Classrooms blend traditional academic subjects—such as language, math, art and cultural studies—with practical life skills that foster independence, concentration, and social responsibility.

Secret Garden’s mission is to cultivate compassionate, critical thinkers who are holistically prepared for the future. This commitment extends beyond the classroom walls through active community involvement. Students participate in gardening, service projects, and local initiatives that build meaningful connections with the surrounding environment. As the school looks toward the future, it continues to explore long-term facility plans, which could include expanding within its current location or eventually pursuing a larger permanent home in the area. For families interested in joining the community, the school maintains waiting lists for both its Toddler and Primary programs, with tuition assistance and scholarship opportunities available for eligible families.

Whether they are tending to gardens, exploring math concepts with hands-on materials, or playing at the local field, Secret Garden students represent a thriving, thoughtful chapter of Frenchtown’s educational landscape. For more information on Secret Garden or to sign your child up for programs, contact info@secretgardenmontessori.org.

FES Centennial Celebration Recap

🔗

Frenchtown Elementary School (FES) held its centennial celebration on October 3. Previously scheduled for September 28, the event was postponed due to inclement weather conditions.

The event kicked off with an opening ceremony featuring the FES band and choir directed by music teacher Peggy Ippolito and an introduction from Kate Nugent, Board of Education President. The band played a series of pep tunes and the choir followed up with singing FES alma mater “Hail Frenchtown!” written by Audrey Kish-Kiley.

Michelle Liebtag, Borough Council President, Juan Torres, Hunterdon County Interim Super-intendent, Jim Hintenach, Chief School Administrator and Sandy Miller, Centennial Coordinator and Secretary of FES all made remarks.

Kathy Skerbetz, a retired preschool teacher’s aide, honored her great aunt Edith Ort Thomas. Ort Thomas was a math teacher and board member who served FES for over 50 years. In 1976, the school was renamed Edith Ort Thomas Elementary School in honor of her years of dedication.

Seventh grade students Avielle McAllister and Ardiana Berisha shared the history of the Spirit of the American Doughboy statue in front of the school. The statue is a memorial to Frenchtown men who served in World War I. The Doughboy was created by E. M. Viquesney, a Spanish-American War veteran and sculptor from Indiana. The sculpture represents a World War 1 infantryman and is one of eight versions of the statue in New Jersey.

“Together our school and the Doughboy remind us that history is not only something we remember; it is something we carry forward,” said McCallister.

Memorabilia was arranged in the cafeteria and guests were invited to view the collection. Collected pieces included graduation pictures from when FES was a high school, yearbooks, letters and newspaper clippings that were either donated by alumni or part of FES’s vast collection.

Rick Epstein’s newly released book Terriers Forever was available for purchase. “One of our FES parents, Natasha Mikhalchuk, worked with the late Rick Epstein to write our book,” said Sandy Miller.

The centennial proved to be a great way to engage the community and demonstrate the importance of FES to Frenchtown. —Amelia Rodriguez

Stink Bugs: A Friendly Houseguest

🔗

This time of year, many folks are vexed by large brown-colored stink bugs trying to get into their houses. This is a fairly new problem—caused by an invasive species, the Brown Marmorated Stink Bug (Halyomorpha halys). This Asian insect was accidentally introduced into the United States and first found in North America nearby in Allentown, PA, in 1996 (having likely been introduced on packing material from overseas). Since then, this insect has spread throughout much of the United States and become a pest of various fruits, field crops and ornamental plants.

Adult Marmorated Stink Bugs are fairly easy to identify—they are just under an inch long and shaped like a shield. The adults enter houses in the fall simply looking for a protected space to spend the winter. Because they feed exclusively on plants, they cannot harm people or pets. As a matter of fact, they scarcely feed during the winter months and do not reproduce—but they certainly can become a nuisance: walking around, being crushed underfoot, flying to lights. Like all stink bugs, they only emit a foul smell when disturbed or crushed. Because of the smell, it is best not to crush them. To move them, you can gently catch them for release outside or vacuum them up (although vacuuming them may cause a stink!).

It should be noted that our native stink bugs are, by and large, much better behaved and do not try to enter our houses in the winter. Hunterdon County is home to quite a few native species of (characteristically shield shaped) stink bugs: the Green, Brown, Juniper, Harlequin, Anchor, Twice Stabbed, Elf Shoe, Tuxedo, Spined Soldier and others. Most of these also feed by sucking plant sap through their piercing-sucking mouthparts, and a few of them, like the Spined Soldier Bugs, are predatory on other insects. But all of these native insects go about their lives in our gardens and woodlands without us ever noticing—it is only the introduced Marmorated Stink Bug that invades our houses in the fall.

—Randi Eckel
